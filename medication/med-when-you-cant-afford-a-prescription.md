## Do this now
**Before you skip, split, or space out doses, call the prescriber or pharmacist and say you can't afford it.** Some medicines are dangerous to stop suddenly. There is often a cheaper option.

## Steps
1. **Tell the prescriber and pharmacist the price is the problem.** Ask:
   - Is there a **generic**? FDA says generics have the same active ingredient and work the same way as the brand, and tend to cost less.
   - Is there a cheaper medicine that works the same way?
   - Would a **90-day supply** cost less?
   - Is there a cheaper pharmacy, or a discount price without insurance?
2. **Don't stop on your own.** FDA warnings say:
   - **Benzodiazepines:** stopping suddenly or cutting the dose too fast can cause withdrawal, including **seizures that can be life-threatening**.
   - **Beta-blockers** (some blood-pressure and heart drugs): stopping suddenly can make chest pain worse or trigger a heart attack.
   - **Seizure medicines:** stopping suddenly can bring more seizures, including nonstop seizures.
   - **Antidepressants:** MedlinePlus (sertraline, for example) says not to stop without talking to your doctor. Stopping suddenly can cause withdrawal symptoms such as nausea, dizziness, anxiety, confusion, and even seizures.
   - Many other medicines also need a plan from the prescriber to stop.
   - Some medicines can be paused only with the prescriber's OK. Ask; don't guess.
3. **Ask about patient assistance.** Many drug makers run assistance programs for people who can't pay. The prescriber's office or pharmacist can help you apply.
4. **Use a community health center.** Federally funded health centers can't turn you away because you can't pay, and they offer sliding-fee discounts based on income and family size (HRSA). Find one at **findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov**.
5. **Lost your job or insurance?** Apply for **Medicaid** through your state, or check **HealthCare.gov** for Marketplace coverage. One HealthCare.gov application also checks whether you qualify for Medicaid or CHIP. If you lost job-based coverage in the past 60 days (or will in the next 60), you may qualify for a Special Enrollment Period.
6. **Controlled medicines (federal law):**
   - A **Schedule II** prescription (for example, many ADHD stimulants and strong opioid pain medicines) **can't be refilled**. Each fill needs a new prescription.
   - A prescriber may write several prescriptions at once, up to a **90-day supply**, each with an earliest fill date, where state law allows.

## If / then
- **If** you're about to run out **then** call the prescriber now, not on the last day. Ask the pharmacist about an emergency supply (see the getting-medication card).
- **If** you already stopped a medicine and feel sick, shaky, or very anxious, or have chest pain or a seizure **then** call the prescriber, or **911** for chest pain, a seizure, or trouble breathing.
- **If** the cost is causing a crisis **then** call **988** or **2-1-1**.

## Don't
- **Never use someone else's medicine,** and never give or sell yours. Federal law bars giving or selling controlled medicines to anyone else (21 U.S.C. 841).
- Don't skip or space doses without asking the prescriber first.
- Don't buy medicine from websites that don't require a prescription.

## Get help now
**911** for emergencies. **Poison Help: 1-800-222-1222**. **2-1-1** for local help paying for medicine. **988** if you're in crisis. Health centers: findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov.
