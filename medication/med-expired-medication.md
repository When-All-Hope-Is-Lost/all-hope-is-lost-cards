## Do this now
**Don't use expired medicine if you can get an in-date supply.** Ask your pharmacist whether a refill or replacement is possible. For a life-threatening problem, call **911**.

## Steps
1. **What FDA says:** after the expiration date there is **no guarantee** a medicine is safe and effective. Expired medicine can be weaker or chemically changed, some can grow bacteria, and weak antibiotics can fail to treat an infection and add to antibiotic resistance. FDA's advice: **if it's expired, don't use it.**
2. **What the federal shelf-life testing found (SLEP):** since 1986, FDA has tested certain federally stockpiled drugs in a program run by the Department of Defense. It is limited to federal stockpiles stored under controlled conditions. Across 122 products (3,005 lots), 88% of lots passed testing for at least a year past the label date, about 66 months on average, but results **varied widely** from lot to lot, and 18% of extended lots later failed. This applies only to drugs **kept sealed in the original container under controlled storage**, and each lot was lab-tested. You can't tell from looking at a bottle at home.
3. **Do not rely on expired supplies of these** (they treat emergencies or infections, or a weak dose is dangerous): insulin, nitroglycerin, antibiotics (especially liquids), epinephrine auto-injectors, and eye drops. **Tetracycline:** degraded tetracycline caused kidney damage in a 1963 report. That formulation is no longer sold, but it's the classic warning case.
4. **Store medicine so it lasts to its date (FDA):** read the label first; some need the fridge and some can't take heat. For most, use a **cool, dry place** like a dresser drawer, storage box, closet shelf, or a kitchen cabinet away from the stove and sink. **Not the bathroom:** damp air can weaken medicine. Keep it out of children's reach.
5. **Get rid of expired or unused medicine (FDA):**
   - **Best:** a drug take-back kiosk (many pharmacies and police stations) or a prepaid mail-back envelope. Find sites through DEA by ZIP code.
   - **If none:** check FDA's **Flush List**. Flush only medicines on that list.
   - **Otherwise, the trash:** mix pills (don't crush them) with dirt, cat litter, or used coffee grounds, seal in a plastic bag, and scratch personal information off the label.

## If / then
- **If** someone is having anaphylaxis and the only epinephrine auto-injector is expired **then** call **911**. One study (2000) found expired injectors deliver less epinephrine; its authors said an outdated one could still be used if the liquid isn't discolored and has no particles, because some epinephrine beats none. Replace injectors before they expire.
- **If** your insulin is expired, or opened longer than its label allows **then** see the insulin card and get a replacement as soon as you can.
- **If** you can't afford a replacement **then** see the can't-afford card.

## Don't
- Don't take someone else's medicine, expired or not.
- Don't flush medicine that isn't on FDA's Flush List.
- Don't store medicine in the bathroom, a hot car, or a glovebox.

## Get help now
**911** for emergencies. **Poison Help: 1-800-222-1222** if someone took the wrong or too much medicine. FDA drug info: **1-888-463-6332**.
