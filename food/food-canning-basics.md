## Do this now
**Use a tested USDA or NCHFP recipe for every jar, with its exact time, pressure and altitude adjustment.** Low-acid foods (vegetables, meat, beans) **must be pressure canned**. A boiling-water bath can't make them safe.

## Steps
1. **Know the acid line, pH 4.6 (USDA):**
   - **Acid foods** (pH 4.6 or lower) can go in a **boiling-water canner**: fruits, pickles, sauerkraut, jams and jellies.
   - **Low-acid foods** (above 4.6) need a **pressure canner**: red meats, seafood, poultry, milk, and **all fresh vegetables** except most tomatoes. Mixtures are usually low-acid too.
2. **Why:** botulism spores are on most fresh food and survive boiling. In a sealed, moist, low-acid jar they can make a deadly toxin. Low-acid foods must reach **240 to 250°F**, which takes a pressure canner at 10 to 15 PSI (USDA).
3. **Acidify tomatoes, every jar (USDA Guide 3):**
   - Per **quart:** 2 tablespoons bottled lemon juice or ½ teaspoon citric acid.
   - Per **pint:** 1 tablespoon bottled lemon juice or ¼ teaspoon citric acid.
   - Don't can tomatoes from dead or frost-killed vines.
4. **Adjust for altitude.** At **1,000 ft or more**, sea-level times can let food spoil (USDA):
   - **Boiling-water canner:** use the recipe's longer time for your altitude.
   - **Weighted-gauge pressure canner:** above 1,000 ft, run at 15 PSI instead of 10 (or 10 instead of 5).
   - **Dial-gauge canner:** use the recipe's pressure for your altitude band, and **have the gauge checked every year**. Replace it if it reads more than 2 lb off.
   - Don't know your altitude? Ask the county extension office.
5. **Vent every pressure canner for 10 minutes** before you pressurize it. Trapped air lowers the temperature (USDA).
6. **Leave the headspace** the recipe gives. Fill jars and wipe the rims.
7. **Check seals once jars are cool.** The lid should be curved down and shouldn't spring back. If a jar didn't seal, reprocess it within 24 hours with a new lid, or refrigerate it and eat it within several days (USDA).
8. **Store:** remove the screw bands, then label and date the jars. Keep them **cool, dark and dry, ideally 50 to 70°F, never above 95°F**. Keep them away from furnaces, stoves, under-sink areas, attics and sun. **Can only what you'll use within a year** (USDA).

## If / then
- **If** a lid bulges, the seal is broken, liquid spurts, it smells off, or you see mold or rising bubbles **then don't taste it.** Treat any low-acid or tomato jar as possibly containing botulinum toxin (USDA).
  - **Sealed** suspect jar: put it in a heavy garbage bag, close the bag, and throw it out.
  - **Unsealed or leaking** jar: wear gloves and boil the jars, lids and food, covered by 1 inch of water, for **30 minutes**, then discard everything.
  - **Clean up:** wipe surfaces with 1 part unscented bleach to 5 parts water, leave for 30 minutes, then repeat. Toxin can enter through skin as well as by mouth (USDA Guide 1).
- **If** someone ate home-canned food and has double or blurred vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, trouble swallowing or breathing, or weakness **then call 911 now.** Botulism is a medical emergency (CDC).
- **If** you don't have a pressure canner **then** freeze, dry or pickle low-acid foods by tested recipes instead. Never water-bath them.

## Don't
- Don't use open-kettle canning, ovens, microwaves or dishwashers to process jars (USDA).
- Don't use "canning powders," zinc caps with rubber rings, or wire-bail glass-lid jars for canning (USDA).
- Don't use steam canners for these recipes; USDA guide (2015) does not recommend them pending research.
- Don't invent or "eyeball" times, and don't use family recipes that have no tested process.

## Get help now
Possible botulism: **911**. Poison Help: **1-800-222-1222**. Recipe and altitude questions: your county extension office or nchfp.uga.edu.
