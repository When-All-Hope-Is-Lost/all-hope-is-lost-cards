## Do this now
**If ramen, pasta, hot dogs, or oats are all you can afford, eat them, but add an egg or beans and a fruit or vegetable to as many meals as you can.** Call **2-1-1** today for food pantries and meals.

## Steps
1. **Start from the cheapest staples.** Rice and pasta are budget-friendly grains (USDA). Plain old-fashioned oatmeal is a whole grain. Ramen noodles and pasta count only as grains: on their own they leave out the other food groups (protein, vegetables, fruit, dairy).
2. **Add cheap protein.** USDA calls **beans, peas, lentils, and eggs** low-cost protein foods. Beans cost less than a similar amount of other protein foods. Canned tuna, salmon, or sardines store well and cost little.
   - Crack an egg into ramen or pasta and cook it until the yolk and white are firm.
   - Stir canned or cooked beans into rice, pasta, or soup.
3. **Add vegetables to bulk up meals.** Frozen vegetables without added sauces or butter are as good for you as fresh and may cost less (USDA). Frozen spinach and green beans are low-cost options. Canned vegetables marked "low-sodium" or "no salt added" are just as nutritious.
4. **Get some vitamin C every day you can.** Fruits and vegetables are the best sources (NIH): citrus fruits and juices, peppers, broccoli, tomatoes, baked potatoes, strawberries, cantaloupe. Some foods are fortified, so check labels. Cooking and long storage lower vitamin C; steaming or microwaving loses less.
5. **Hot dogs:** they're fully cooked, but heat them until steaming hot if anyone eating is pregnant, older, or has a weak immune system (listeria risk). Refrigerate or freeze them right after you buy them. With no date on the pack, keep them unopened 2 weeks in the fridge, or 1 week once opened.
6. **Check the Nutrition Facts label for sodium** on ramen, hot dogs, and canned foods, and pick lower-sodium choices when you can. USDA advises limiting sodium.
7. **Sample day (estimate, not a USDA plan):** oatmeal for breakfast; ramen with an egg and frozen vegetables for lunch; rice and beans with canned tomatoes for supper. That's about 1 oz of protein from the egg and 2 oz from each ½ cup of beans, against roughly 5½ oz a day for a 2,000-calorie diet (MyPlate).
8. **Compare unit prices** on the shelf tag, and buy what keeps: whole grains, dried or canned beans, frozen vegetables.

## If / then
- **If** you can only afford one add-on **then** pick eggs or beans for protein, and frozen vegetables when you can.
- **If** you can't get any fruit or vegetables for a long stretch **then** look for vitamin C-fortified foods and ask a food pantry for produce. Too little vitamin C over time causes **scurvy** (tiredness, weak connective tissue, fragile blood vessels) (NIH).
- **If** your power is out or you have no fridge **then** skip hot dogs and eggs. Use canned beans, canned fish, oats, and canned vegetables instead.
- **If** you get SNAP **then** many farmers markets accept it (USDA).

## Don't
- Don't leave cooked eggs, hot dogs, or leftovers out longer than **2 hours** (**1 hour** above 90°F).
- Don't eat runny eggs. Cook them until the yolk and white are firm.
- Don't eat cracked eggs. Throw them out.
- Don't skip meals for kids to save money. Ask a pantry, 2-1-1, or the school about free meals first.

## Get help now
**2-1-1** for food pantries and free meals. SNAP: fns.usda.gov/snap/state-directory. **988** if money stress is overwhelming. **911** for emergencies.
